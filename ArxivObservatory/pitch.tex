\head{The phenomenon.}
In an eight-day census of all new \textsf{math.AG} arXiv submissions (June--July 2026, $n=129$), 13 papers (10\%) explicitly disclosed AI assistance --- from grammar polishing to an abstract stating that the main result of the paper was obtained by a combination of AI systems. Existing broad measurements see different slices of this: word-frequency methods estimate up to 9\% LLM-modified \emph{prose} in mathematics (\href{https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-025-02273-8}{Nature Hum.\ Behav.\ 2025}), in-text-evidence mining finds 1.3\% documented foundation-model \emph{use} in math in 2024 (\ax{2511.21739}; a broad model family incl.\ vision models), and 81\% of survey \emph{respondents} (self-selected sample) report LLM use somewhere in their workflow (\ax{2411.05025}). These are different estimands, and none is mathematics-specific at full-text depth. We want to measure the transition properly, while it happens.

\head{Questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Adoption and epistemic role.} How do mathematics papers use LLMs, computer algebra, custom code, and proof assistants --- and in what role: \emph{cosmetic} (prose, translation), \emph{supportive} (search, code, computations, examples, proof criticism), or \emph{result-bearing} (conjectures, retained proof steps, counterexamples, formal verification)? How does this evolve across time, subfield, and arXiv version history?
  \item \textbf{Disclosure vs.\ detection.} How do explicit writing-related disclosures relate to detector-inferred LLM modification of mathematical prose, once the detector is calibrated on pre-LLM mathematics and on controlled positives (grammar-only editing, translation, rewriting) by section type?
  \item \textbf{Reliability.} What lower bound does a preregistered AI-plus-expert audit place on the fraction of pure-math paper-versions containing at least one independently confirmed substantive error --- reported as cumulative severity levels (local slip $\to$ repairable gap $\to$ statement false), never a single ``$X$\% of papers are wrong''?
\end{enumerate}

\head{Method.}
For disclosures, we combine deterministic parsers with application-specific keyword detection on \LaTeX{} sources, LLM scans, and human review --- cross-validated against each other on shared gold samples, so that class-specific precision \emph{and recall} are known and prevalence estimates carry honest uncertainty rather than raw keyword counts (prototyped on the pilot). For AI-writing detection, Pangram runs on (subsets of) paper texts, calibrated on pre-LLM mathematics and on controlled positives (grammar-only editing, translation, rewriting). For error detection, frontier review agents --- and possibly the community workflow below --- propose flags that pass independent critique and double expert adjudication; a known-positive benchmark built from errata and version diffs (cf.\ \ax{2605.20531}) measures rediscovery separately from the blinded representative sample ($\sim$100--300 papers) used for prevalence. All three strands run on iterative stratified samples: a rough picture with explicit error bars emerges quickly, then is refined and updated as data comes in, with the arXiv paper-version as the unit of analysis. (Full texts come from arXiv's sanctioned bulk channels --- no scraping, no server load.)

\head{Positioning.}
Already done elsewhere: cross-disciplinary acknowledgment counts ($\sim$80\% concern editing; \href{https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11192-024-05193-y}{Scientometrics 2024}); population-level prose estimates; Pangram sampled on arXiv with pre-LLM calibration on CS only (\ax{2601.17036}); a disclosure--detector comparison in medicine (\ax{2603.19316}); a GPT-5 error audit of AI-conference papers (83\% flag precision, 60\% seeded recall; \ax{2512.05925}); an unvalidated mass audit of math papers (\ax{2511.10543}). Open --- and our contribution: a mathematics-specific, cross-tool, version-aware taxonomy with epistemic-role coding; detector calibration on mathematical prose; the disclosure$\times$detection cross-tab for mathematics restricted to roles a text detector can plausibly see; and a representative, expert-confirmed error lower bound.

\head{Work packages and deliverables.}
\textbf{WP1} Disclosed-assistance observatory: open taxonomy, human-annotated gold corpus, validated extraction pipeline, longitudinal estimates by subfield and role on 5{,}000--20{,}000 paper-versions, living dashboard. \textbf{WP2} Calibrated detector study: pre-LLM math control corpus, controlled positives, section-wise false-positive rates, disclosure--detector overlap. \textbf{WP3} Reliability audit (a separate, expert-heavy paper): severity rubric, known-error benchmark, model precision/recall studies, aggregate lower bound. All code and annotations released openly (IDs and hashes rather than full text, per arXiv licensing).

\head{A community experiment.}
Much of the expensive frontier-model pass could be crowd-sourced to mathematicians who already hold model subscriptions: a website assigns you a specific arXiv paper; you drop it into your model with a fixed prompt and paste back the conversation share-link --- optionally also ticking a few checkboxes about the paper's acknowledgments. Share-links let us audit that the intended model and prompt were used; double-assignment and seeded known-error papers guard against careless or adversarial contributions. Nearly free, doubles as outreach, and gives the community a stake in the dataset.

\head{Practicalities and ethics.}
WP1 plus all covariate analysis costs approximately nothing (metadata dumps, small models, existing subscriptions); WP2 is a modest API budget or a collaboration with Pangram Labs (who have co-authored comparable studies); WP3 is lab-credit scale, bottlenecked by expert time rather than tokens. Throughout: aggregate reporting only, no per-paper accusations, detector output framed as ``detector-inferred LLM modification'' --- a measurement, never evidence of misconduct --- and authors are contacted before any confirmed error becomes public.

\head{We are looking for.}
Mathematicians across subfields for annotation and error adjudication (a few hours each); early volunteers to pilot the community workflow; a statistics/metascience collaborator; contacts at Pangram Labs or detector expertise; compute credits. Pilot data, prior-art survey, and technical notes are available on request.
